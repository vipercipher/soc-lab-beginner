# Day 20 — Mini Case: Putting It All Together

📅 30 October 2026 · 🏷️ Mini case · ⏱️ ~35 min

## Why it matters

Every entry so far has handed you one skill at a time: logon events
(Day 01), phishing headers (Day 08), process trees (Days 05–07), DNS
(Day 12), persistence (Day 15), triage (Day 17), the IR lifecycle (Day 18)
and tickets (Day 19). A real queue doesn't arrive labelled by day. Today is
one morning at Northwind, **Wednesday 28 October**, worked from the first
alert to the first ticket. Nothing here is new; the skill being practised is
**joining the pieces up**, quickly and in the right order, the way Scenario
01's `daniel.cole` case did for a single alert.

## The concept

### Working a case end to end

| Step | Question | Where you learned it | Today's evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Triage the queue** | Which alerts belong together, and what first? | Days 10, 17 | 9 alerts, 3 users |
| **2. Delivery** | How did it get in, and who else received it? | Days 08, 09 | Message trace |
| **3. Execution** | What ran, started by what? | Days 05, 06, 07 | Process events, 4104 |
| **4. Command and control** | Is it talking out? | Days 09, 12, 13 | DNS alerts |
| **5. Persistence** | Will it survive a reboot? | Days 14, 15, 16 | Scheduled task |
| **6. Scope** | Which hosts, accounts and mailboxes? | Days 01, 17 | Who received, who opened |
| **7. Respond and record** | Contain, eradicate, recover; write it down | Days 18, 19 | Your plan and ticket |

> 💡 **The analyst's shortcut:** at every step, ask *"what's the next link
> in the chain, and which log would show it?"* Email shows delivery, the
> endpoint shows execution, DNS and the firewall show C2. A gap in the chain
> is either a missing log or the next thing to look for.

### Correlating across data sources

The hard part of a multi-stage case is that each log names the victim
differently:

| Data source | Table | Who | Where |
|---|---|---|---|
| Email (Defender for Office 365) | `EmailEvents`, `EmailAttachmentInfo` | `RecipientEmailAddress` | No host |
| Endpoint (Defender for Endpoint) | `DeviceProcessEvents` | `AccountUpn`, `AccountName` | `DeviceName` |
| Sysmon (Days 05, 12) | Event IDs 1, 22 | `User` (`NORTHWIND\grace.liu`) | `Computer` |
| Alerts (Day 10) | `northwind_alerts` | `user` (`grace.liu`) | `host` (empty for email alerts) |

At Northwind every user's sign-in name (UPN) is the same as their email
address, so `grace.liu@northwind-example.com` joins email to endpoint. That
one shared key is what turns two separate alerts into one story.

## What to look for

1. **Nine alerts, three users, one incident.** `grace.liu` has **7**
   (A-1302 and A-1304 to A-1309), `ben.okafor` **1** and `tom.reyes` **1**.
   Grouped by user (Day 10), Grace's alerts cover **4** tactics in
   **17 min 50 s** (00:52:10 → 01:10:00): Initial Access (NW-006) →
   Execution (NW-005, NW-004) → Command and Control (NW-007, NW-010,
   NW-009) → Persistence (NW-016). Day 10's meta-detection fires;
   `ben.okafor` and `tom.reyes` have **1** tactic each.
2. **The host roll-up agrees.** In Day 17's triage helper, `WS-FIN05` has
   **6** alerts from **6** different rules, so it's **P1** on the
   "3 or more rules" test alone. That matters, because `WS-FIN05` **isn't**
   in `northwind_assets.csv`: its criticality comes out `unknown`, a gap to
   fix in the asset list.
3. **A lookalike that passes every check.** `fabrikarn-supplies.example`
   uses **rn** to look like the **m** in `fabrikam`. SPF, DKIM and DMARC all
   **pass** because the attacker owns that domain (Day 08): *pass* means
   "really sent by this domain", not "safe". **2** recipients got the same
   message (same `NetworkMessageId`, `d3a8…`), so scope starts at two
   mailboxes, not one.
4. **New IOCs, old behaviour.** The domain, the sender IP `203.0.113.71`,
   the download server `198.51.100.171` and the hash `B91E…07D4` are all
   new, so Day 09's block list from 15 October caught nothing. The **same
   playbook** as Days 07–12 (invoice-style `.docm` → Word → hidden encoded
   PowerShell → download → `rundll32` → DGA-style lookups → beacon) was
   caught by **behaviour** rules. That's the Pyramid of Pain, live.
5. **Sixteen seconds from click to code.** Word opened the attachment at
   01:03:46 and started PowerShell at 01:04:02. `-W Hidden -enc` (Day 06)
   and a Word parent (Day 07) are both red flags, and the 4104 shows what
   the Base64 was hiding: a download into `%TEMP%`. Three seconds after
   PowerShell started, `rundll32.exe` loaded `ra30418.dat`, a file with a `.dat` name being run
   as a DLL.
6. **It planned to come back.** At 01:06:30 the malware created a daily
   task, `OneDriveSyncHelper`: a name chosen to look like Microsoft
   software, pointing into `\AppData\Local\Temp\` (Day 15). Isolating the
   laptop stops the beacon; only **eradication** removes the task.
7. **Ruling things out is half the job.** `ben.okafor` has **no** process
   events: the email arrived but nothing ran, which matches his report. Priya's
   `.xlsm` is an internal file from a colleague, and Excel's only child was
   `splwow64.exe`, the print helper (Day 07). Tom's A-1301 matches
   CHG-2230's day, host and time: a **benign positive** (Day 17).
8. **One step had no rule of its own.** PowerShell starting `rundll32.exe`
   on a `.dat` file in `%TEMP%` raised nothing; Grace's chain was caught
   anyway, but only because the steps before and after it fired. That's a
   Day 10 coverage gap worth writing down.

## Detection logic

**In plain English:** link email to endpoint. Alert when a user is
**delivered** an email with a **macro-enabled Office attachment**, and
within **30 minutes** an Office application running as **that same user**
starts a shell or script host. Either half alone is noisy (finance swaps
`.xlsm` files all day; Day 07's rule fires on some admin macros); together
they describe the exact chain in this case.

**KQL (Microsoft Defender XDR advanced hunting, or Microsoft Sentinel with
the Defender XDR connector):**

```kql
// Macro attachment delivered, then Office spawns a shell for the same user within 30 min
let window = 30m;
let lookback = 1d;
let shells = dynamic(["cmd.exe", "powershell.exe", "pwsh.exe", "wscript.exe",
                      "cscript.exe", "mshta.exe", "rundll32.exe"]);
let macroMail =
    EmailAttachmentInfo
    | where Timestamp > ago(lookback)
    | where FileName endswith ".docm" or FileName endswith ".xlsm" or FileName endswith ".pptm"
    | join kind=inner (
        EmailEvents
        | where Timestamp > ago(lookback)
        | where DeliveryAction == "Delivered"
        | project NetworkMessageId, RecipientEmailAddress, SenderFromDomain, Subject
      ) on NetworkMessageId, RecipientEmailAddress
    | project EmailTime = Timestamp, Upn = tolower(RecipientEmailAddress),
              SenderFromDomain, Subject, Attachment = FileName;
DeviceProcessEvents
| where Timestamp > ago(lookback)
| where InitiatingProcessFileName in~ ("winword.exe", "excel.exe", "powerpnt.exe")
| where FileName in~ (shells)
| extend Upn = tolower(AccountUpn)
| join kind=inner macroMail on Upn
| where Timestamp between (EmailTime .. (EmailTime + window))
| project EmailTime, ProcessTime = Timestamp, DeviceName, Upn, SenderFromDomain,
          Attachment, InitiatingProcessFileName, FileName, ProcessCommandLine
```

On the sample it returns **1** row:

```
EmailTime            ProcessTime          DeviceName  Upn                              SenderFromDomain            Attachment             InitiatingProcessFileName  FileName
2026-10-28 00:52:07  2026-10-28 01:04:02  WS-FIN05    grace.liu@northwind-example.com  fabrikarn-supplies.example  Remittance_30418.docm  WINWORD.EXE                powershell.exe
```

The shell started **11 min 55 s** after delivery, inside the 30-minute
window. Priya's `.xlsm` is in `macroMail`, but Excel only started
`splwow64.exe`, which isn't on the `shells` list. Ben's copy is in
`macroMail` too, but no Office process ran under his account. The other
process rows have `OUTLOOK.EXE` or `powershell.exe` as the parent, so the
Office filter drops them.

How it works:

- **One email, many rows.** `EmailEvents` and `EmailAttachmentInfo` have a
  row **per recipient**, and Grace's and Ben's copies share one
  `NetworkMessageId`. Joining on **both** `NetworkMessageId` and
  `RecipientEmailAddress` keeps each person's attachment matched to their
  own delivery result.
- `DeliveryAction == "Delivered"` drops messages that were `Blocked` or
  `Junked`: a quarantined attachment can't be opened from the inbox.
- `tolower()` on both sides makes `Grace.Liu@…` and `grace.liu@…` match,
  because `join` compares strings exactly. `in~` is the case-insensitive
  `in` (Day 18), so `WINWORD.EXE` matches `winword.exe`.
- `kind=inner` keeps every matching pair. KQL's **default** join
  (`innerunique`) removes duplicate keys on the left side first, which can
  silently hide a second shell process.
- The final `where ... between` keeps only shells that started **after**
  delivery and within the window, as on Day 01.

**Tuning note:** the join depends on **UPN = email address**. Where they
differ (aliases, shared mailboxes, a name change), join on
`RecipientObjectId` and `AccountObjectId` instead, the user's Entra ID
object ID. Users often **save** an attachment and open it later, so a
30-minute window will miss some; a companion version over **24 hours** at
lower severity covers that. If a legitimate macro genuinely starts a shell
(some finance add-ins call `cmd.exe`), allow-list it **narrowly** by the
attachment's `SHA256` or the internal sender, never by file type. Because
`rundll32.exe` and `cmd.exe` are on the list, expect a few hits from
printing and add-in helpers; review them before raising severity. When it
fires, it should be **high**: it is two of Day 10's tactics (Initial Access,
T1566.001, and Execution, T1204.002) linked to one person.
